# Episode: Pakistan Is Selling Its Power Companies — But Not the Ones You'd Expect

## Hook

Pakistan is about to privatize its power distribution companies for the first time in over a
decade. If you had to guess which ones would be sold first, you'd probably guess the worst
ones — the companies bleeding the country dry every single year. You'd be wrong. The government
is selling three of the *better*-performing ones instead. And once you understand why, you'll
understand exactly what's broken in Pakistan's power sector — and what this sale will, and
won't, actually fix.

## Context

Pakistan's power distribution companies — DISCOs — lose money two ways: technical losses
(electricity that leaks out through old wires and overloaded transformers) and non-technical
losses (theft, unpaid bills, meter tampering). Together, this is one of the biggest engines
behind Pakistan's circular debt problem. In FY2024-25 alone, DISCOs lost close to Rs. 397
billion between transmission losses and shortfalls in bill recovery [NEPRA 2025 State of
Industry Report, via Profit by Pakistan Today, 16 Jan 2026].

This year, the government moved to privatize three of them: FESCO (Faisalabad), GEPCO
(Gujranwala), and IESCO (Islamabad) [ProPakistani, 16 July 2026]. Around a dozen investors have
expressed interest, four of them foreign firms [ProPakistani, 16 July 2026]. And this isn't
just a domestic decision — privatizing these DISCOs is one of the conditions tied to unlocking
Pakistan's next IMF loan tranche [ProPakistani, 18 March 2026].

So: why these three? What are the buyers actually demanding in return? And what happened the
last time Pakistan tried something like this?

## Escalating narrative

### The cherry-picking

[cut to: comfort-range graphic — NEPRA limit vs national average]

Here's the number that should surprise you. NEPRA allows DISCOs a maximum transmission and
distribution loss of 11.43%. Nationally, DISCOs are running at 17.55% — nearly six points over
the limit [NEPRA 2025 State of Industry Report]. But look at the three companies actually being
sold: NEPRA's own performance data puts IESCO's losses at just 8.6% — already *under* the
regulatory limit [NEPRA Performance Evaluation Report, FY2024-25]. ProPakistani reports FESCO
and GEPCO were selected for the same reason — combined losses in the 9-10% range, which
regulators consider manageable [ProPakistani, 16 July 2026].

Now compare that to the DISCOs staying in government hands. PESCO in Peshawar is losing 37.1%
of the electricity it distributes. SEPCO in Sukkur is losing 39.2% [NEPRA 2025 State of Industry
Report]. Those two companies alone account for a huge share of the country's entire DISCO
losses.

So if the goal were to fix the biggest problem, the government would be selling PESCO and
SEPCO first. It's selling the DISCOs that were already closest to functioning normally.

### Why buyers won't touch the real problem

[cut to: buyer-demands graphic]

This stops being a mystery once you look at it from the buyer's side. Reporting from Profit by
Pakistan Today shows that even for these three relatively healthy DISCOs, investors are
demanding dollar-indexed returns, sovereign guarantees, and an exemption from Pakistan's
expensive long-term power purchase agreements with independent power producers [Profit by
Pakistan Today, 13 Aug 2026]. The government, for its part, is reportedly targeting a 20% rate
of return to make the deal attractive enough [Business Recorder].

Here's why a foreign buyer specifically wants dollar-indexed returns. Say an investor is
promised a 15% annual return, paid in rupees — sounds solid, until you remember the rupee has a
long history of depreciating against the dollar. If the rupee loses 10% of its value against
the dollar that same year, the investor's real return in dollar terms drops to roughly 5%.
*[for illustration only]* That currency risk is shaping almost every term on the table in these
negotiations right now.

Now imagine offering those same terms for PESCO or SEPCO — companies losing nearly 40 cents of
every rupee's worth of electricity they distribute. No investor takes on that math at any
"reasonable" rate of return. The deal that's commercially possible today is, almost by
definition, only possible with the DISCOs that were already closest to healthy.

### The IMF's stopwatch

[cut to: chain diagram — IMF tranche → privatization condition → deal timeline]

There's a second pressure at play. This isn't Pakistan modernizing its power sector on its own
schedule — it's tied directly to the IMF program, with DISCO privatization named as a condition
for releasing the next loan tranche [ProPakistani, 18 March 2026]. That means the government
needs a deal it can actually close, on a deadline it doesn't fully control. A deal you can
close on that kind of timeline looks a lot more like selling DISCOs that are already close to
viable than trying to fix and then sell the ones that are genuinely broken.

### What happened the last time

[cut to: timeline graphic — 2005 to 2012]

There's a real precedent for this kind of deal, and it's worth knowing before assuming
privatization is a quick fix. In December 2005, the government privatized Karachi's power
utility — then called KESC, now K-Electric — selling 73% of it to a Saudi-Kuwaiti consortium
for roughly Rs. 16 billion, about $270 million at the time [Geo News]. Before the sale, the
utility was losing an estimated Rs. 107 billion a year, crippled by chronic political
interference in its management [Geo News]. After the sale, the new owners spent years on
restructuring, system modernization, and expanding generation capacity — and the company didn't
turn a profit until June 2012. Seven years after being sold [Geo News].

That's the realistic timeline for a DISCO turnaround, even under private ownership, even when
it eventually works. Selling FESCO, GEPCO, and IESCO doesn't mean Pakistan's power sector
finances look better next year. If K-Electric is any guide, it means better numbers sometime
years out — if the buyers stay the course that long.

### What actually changes for you

[cut to: presenter, direct address]

So what does this mean for someone living in Islamabad, Gujranwala, or Faisalabad? In theory, a
private operator has a direct financial incentive to fix faulty meters, chase down unpaid
commercial bills, and invest in the grid — every unit of electricity they stop losing goes
straight to their return. But that same operator is also the one demanding a guaranteed 20%
return to take the deal at all. So the realistic version is a mix: better reliability over
time, but tariff structures built to protect that guaranteed return — meaning consumers in
these three cities could see costs shift even as service improves. *[for illustration only —

## Payoff/insight

Put it together and here's the real story: this isn't Pakistan fixing its power distribution
sector — it's Pakistan selling off the three parts of it that were already closest to fixed,
because those are the only parts a buyer, an IMF deadline, and a government can all agree are
sellable right now. PESCO, SEPCO, and the rest of the loss-heavy DISCOs — the actual source of
most of the country's Rs. 397 billion in annual DISCO losses — aren't going anywhere. They'll
keep losing money, keep feeding circular debt, and keep waiting for a fix this deal was never
designed to deliver.
