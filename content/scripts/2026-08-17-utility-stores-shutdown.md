# Episode: Why Pakistan Shut Down Every Utility Store — And Blamed the Losses It Created

## Hook

In July 2025, Pakistan shut down every single Utility Store in the country — a chain that had
sold subsidized flour, sugar, and ghee to low-income families since 1971. The government's
official reason was simple: the stores were losing too much money to keep running. Here's what
that reason leaves out — Utility Stores only started losing money once the government stopped
subsidizing them.

## Context

The Utility Stores Corporation (USC) was created in 1971 with one job: sell essential goods
below the open-market price and act as a price moderator for ordinary households [Wikipedia,
Utility Stores Corporation]. In its biggest expansion, between 2007 and 2009, USC grew from
1,023 outlets to 5,557, and staff grew from roughly 3,900 to nearly 12,750 [Wikipedia]. By 2025
the network had shrunk to somewhere around 3,200 operating stores [Daily Pakistan, 21 Mar 2025]
*[NEEDS SOURCE: reconcile the 5,557-outlet 2009 peak against the ~3,200 figure reported in
2025 — the gap isn't explained in the sources checked so far]*.

Then, in 2025, it all came apart fast. So: what actually killed a program that had survived
five decades and a dozen governments? Why did the losses start exactly when they did? And what
happens now to the roughly 11,600 people who worked there [TechJuice, 2025]?

## Escalating narrative

### A subsidy, not a business

[cut to: mechanism diagram — cost, subsidized price, government reimbursement]

To understand what actually happened, you have to understand that USC was never supposed to
turn a profit like a normal retailer. Say a bag of flour costs USC Rs. 100 to procure and
distribute. USC sells it to a family for Rs. 85. That Rs. 15 gap isn't a loss in the way a
failing business loses money — it's the subsidy doing exactly what it was designed to do, with
the government reimbursing USC for the difference. *[for illustration only]* On paper, that gap
shows up as a "loss." In practice, for most of USC's history, it was simply the cost of the
policy working.

### Who actually pulled the plug

[cut to: before/after graphic — same formula, subsidy removed]

Now run that same Rs. 100/Rs. 85 example forward to 2025 — except this time, take the
reimbursement away. Under Pakistan's $7 billion IMF program, approved in September 2024, the
government committed to fiscal discipline that included winding down blanket, untargeted
subsidies [Profit by Pakistan Today, 5 May 2025]. In its FY2025-26 budget, the government
completely withdrew Utility Stores' subsidy, part of a broader Rs. 190 billion cut to subsidies
overall [ProPakistani, 11 Jun 2025]. Once that reimbursement disappeared, the same Rs. 15 gap
that used to be routine stopped being a policy cost and started being a straight loss on USC's
own books — the exact "loss" the government would later cite as the reason to shut USC down.

### The escalation, month by month

[cut to: timeline graphic, Feb-Jul 2025]

What started as a downsizing plan escalated into total closure within about five months.
In February 2025, USC employees protested plans to close 1,000 stores and terminate 3,000 jobs
[UrduPoint, 2025]. By March, the government confirmed a formal plan: of roughly 3,200 stores,
1,700 loss-making ones would close, with only 1,500 stores kept staffed after a planned
privatization [Daily Pakistan, 21 Mar 2025]. The IMF then directed Pakistan to close 1,000
stores and cut the workforce by June 30, and the layoffs came in waves — 2,237 daily-wage
workers in the first phase, roughly 2,800 contract employees across grades 1 to 13 in the
second [Profit by Pakistan Today, 5 May 2025]. By July 10, the government announced every
remaining store would shut. By July 31, 2025, USC had ceased all operations nationwide — over
1,000 rented outlets and 1,230 franchise stores closed in under two weeks [TechJuice, 2025].

### The bill for closing it

[cut to: comfort-range/shocking-stat graphic]

Here's the number that puts the whole "losses" justification in perspective. The Ministry of
Finance cited a Rs. 6.1 billion operating loss in the first half of FY25, pushing USC's
accumulated losses to Rs. 15.9 billion [TechJuice, 2025]. To close the company down, the
government allocated roughly $108 million — close to Rs. 30 billion at prevailing exchange
rates — including a Rs. 30.2 billion supplementary grant approved by the ECC and Rs. 27 billion
earmarked to clear dues owed to employees and vendors [Arab News, 2025; Profit by Pakistan
Today, 26 & 28 Aug 2025]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: the Rs27bn, Rs28bn/Rs28.2bn, and Rs30.2bn figures
across different reports appear to cover overlapping but not identical items — reconcile
against a single primary Ministry of Finance breakdown before quoting an exact total on
camera.]* Shutting down an institution losing roughly Rs16 billion a year cost the government
nearly double that in a single lump sum.

### Who actually pays now

[cut to: presenter, direct address]

Two different groups absorbed the consequences of this. USC's roughly 11,600 employees were
made eligible for a Voluntary Separation Scheme, with severance funded out of that ~Rs. 28-30
billion package [TechJuice; Photonews, 2025] — a real cost, but a one-time, government-cushioned
one. The other group got nothing comparable: the low-income families who relied on USC for
flour, sugar, and ghee below market price now buy those same items at whatever the open market
charges, with no equivalent program to replace it and no severance of their own. Picture a
household in a small town that used to budget around USC's prices every month — that gap in
their monthly budget didn't get a press release. *[for illustration only]*

## Payoff/insight

The real story here isn't that Utility Stores failed as a business — it was never supposed to
be judged as one. It's that a policy decision to withdraw a subsidy got recast, a few budget
cycles later, as an operational failure, and then used to justify shutting the whole program
down rather than reforming or better targeting it. That's not a one-off pattern in Pakistan
right now — it's the same shape as the story behind the country's circular debt and the
push to privatize its power distribution companies: a subsidy gets pulled under IMF pressure,
the resulting losses get treated as proof the underlying institution failed, and the fix that
follows deals with the institution, not the subsidy question underneath it.
