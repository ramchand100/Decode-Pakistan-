# Episode: How Balochistan Got Here — Resources, Repression, and a Conflict Older Than the Country's Youngest Citizens

*Production note: this traces roughly 77 years across three acts (history, economics, present-day

before recording.*

## Hook

Balochistan has produced more of Pakistan's natural gas than any other province since the
1950s. It also has the lowest access to that same gas of any province in the country
[Grand Review; Project O Five, gas-access reporting]. That contradiction is nearly 70 years
old — and it's only one piece of why an armed insurgency there has now outlasted five separate
phases of conflict, with no clear end in sight. So how did it get here?

## Context

Balochistan is Pakistan's largest province by land area and its richest in natural resources —
gas, coal, copper, gold. It's also its poorest by nearly every human-development measure, with
a poverty rate estimated around 71%, the highest of any province [The Grand Review,
"Balochistan's Development Paradox"]. Since 1948, the region has seen recurring waves of armed
conflict between Baloch nationalist movements and the Pakistani state — four short-lived
uprisings, and a fifth that began in 2006 and hasn't stopped since [Khorasan Diary, "Timeline
of Insurgency in Balochistan"; Georgetown GJIA, "The Balochistan Quagmire"].

This episode traces three things: how the conflict actually started, why a resource-rich
province stayed so poor, and what's happening on the ground right now — as of the most recent
reporting available, in a story that is still actively unfolding.

## Escalating narrative

### Act One: Where the conflict began

[cut to: map, 1948]

In March 1948, the princely state of Kalat — which covered much of what is now Balochistan —
acceded to the newly formed Pakistan. How that accession happened is itself disputed: Baloch
nationalist accounts describe it as a forced annexation of a state that had briefly declared
independence months earlier; the Pakistani government's position is that the Khan of Kalat's
accession was constitutional and voluntary [background synthesized across Khorasan Diary and
CTC West Point, "The Baloch Insurgency in Pakistan"]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: this framing needs a
direct primary/historical source check before recording — both sides' positions should be
attributed precisely, not paraphrased from secondary summaries.]*

What followed were four separate, comparatively short-lived uprisings — beginning in 1948, then
recurring roughly each decade through the 1970s — each centered on demands for greater
political and economic autonomy from the central government [Khorasan Diary; ResearchGate,
"History and Evolution of Insurgency in Balochistan"].

The pattern changed with one man: Akbar Bugti. Bugti wasn't an outsider to the state — he was
head of the Bugti tribe and had served as a federal minister, provincial chief minister, and
governor of Balochistan [Wikipedia, "Bugti militia"]. His break with the center escalated over,
among other things, a dispute involving Pakistan Petroleum Limited — a land lease and
provincial royalty disagreement that Bugti and other Baloch leaders framed as the federal
government profiting from Balochistan's gas while shortchanging the province itself [Herald/
Dawn, "Akbar Bugti's death and the revival of the Baloch insurgency"]. On 26 August 2006, Bugti
was killed in a military operation ordered under President Pervez Musharraf. His death is
widely described by analysts as the spark for the current, ongoing insurgency — the first phase
in nearly two decades that never actually ended [The Diplomat, "Balochistan and the Killing of
Akbar Bugti: 10 Years Later"].

### Act Two: The paradox of a resource-rich province

[cut to: resource map — gas, copper, gold]

Here's the mechanism behind Act One's grievance, made concrete. Balochistan's Sui gas fields
have supplied the rest of Pakistan since the 1950s. At its peak, the province accounted for
over half of Pakistan's total domestic gas production [Grand Review]. And yet Balochistan
itself has the lowest rate of household gas access of any province in the country
[Project O Five, "Gas, Gold, and Grievance"]. The province's own government has long alleged
chronic underpayment and delayed payment of gas royalties by the federal government and
state-run energy enterprises [Project O Five].

The same pattern shows up with Reko Diq — one of the largest undeveloped copper-and-gold
deposits on Earth. In 2011, Balochistan's provincial government cancelled a mining contract
with Tethyan Copper Company. In 2019, an international arbitration panel hit Pakistan with a
$5.97 billion damages award over that cancellation [Grand Review, sourced arbitration figures].
A renegotiated 2022 agreement brought Barrick Gold back in under new terms: the mine is now 50%
owned by Barrick, 25% by federal enterprises, and 25% by the Balochistan provincial government
itself [Grand Review]. That 25% is real progress on paper — but it also means three-quarters of
one of the world's largest gold-and-copper deposits, sitting inside Balochistan, belongs to
parties other than the province it's located in.

Gwadar tells the same story from a different angle. The port and its surrounding special
economic zone have been publicly positioned as the flagship of CPEC — the anchor project meant
to bring jobs, trade, and development to Balochistan specifically [Modern Diplomacy, "Can the
Military Force Solve a Political Problem?"]. But residents of Gwadar itself are reported to
still lack reliable access to clean drinking water, healthcare, and schools [Modern Diplomacy].
And the government's dominant response to unrest around the port hasn't been to address that
benefit-sharing gap — it's been a dramatic increase in security presence [Modern Diplomacy].

### Act Three: Where things stand right now

[cut to: timeline graphic, late 2025 into 2026]

This is the most current, and the most contested, part of the story — and the sourcing matters
here more than anywhere else in this episode. According to annual tallies compiled by Baloch
rights organizations — the Baloch Yakjehti Committee (BYC) and Paank, the human rights wing of
the Baloch National Movement — 1,355 people were forcibly disappeared and 225 reportedly killed
under unclear circumstances in Balochistan during 2025 alone [BYC/Paank tallies, via reporting
compiled by ajmals.substack and Balochwarna News]. *[These figures come from Baloch nationalist
advocacy organizations, not an independent government or UN body — the Pakistani state disputes
many individual cases and often characterizes disappeared or killed individuals as militants.
NEEDS SOURCE: cross-check against Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch's independent
reporting, and seek any official government response or rebuttal, before treating these
figures as settled on camera.]* By the same organizations' count, the trend accelerated into
early 2026: 106 new disappearance cases in November 2025, 90 in December, 107 in January 2026,
and 234 in February 2026 [same sourcing].

On the other side of this cycle: on 31 January, the Baloch Liberation Army — an armed separatist
group designated a terrorist organization by Pakistan and several other governments — carried
out coordinated attacks across more than a dozen Balochistan cities, including the provincial
capital, Quetta [reporting compiled via HRC Balochistan fact sheets]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: confirm
the exact year of this attack and cross-check against a primary wire report before recording —
sourcing here comes from an advocacy-aligned aggregator and needs independent confirmation.]*

Independent analysts — not just advocacy groups — describe what's happening as a
self-reinforcing cycle: heavy-handed security responses to insurgent violence are reported to
be fueling further recruitment into groups like the BLA and the Balochistan Liberation Front,
rather than reducing them [Georgetown GJIA, "The Balochistan Quagmire: A Cycle of Violence,
Political Mobilization, and Repression"; CTC West Point].

## Payoff/insight

None of these three acts stands alone. A contested 1948 accession created a lasting grievance
about political voice. Decades of resource extraction without proportional local benefit turned
that grievance into an economic one — Balochistan produces the gas and hosts the gold it can't
fully access or profit from. And a security-first response to the resulting unrest is, according
to independent analysts as well as rights groups, deepening the very cycle it's meant to end,
not resolving it. This isn't a story with a clean villain or a clean fix. It's a case study in
how political exclusion, economic extraction, and security escalation can each make the other
two worse — and why nearly 80 years in, that cycle hasn't been broken by any side of it.
