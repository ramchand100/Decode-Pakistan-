# Episode: Pakistan's Provinces Get Rs. 8.2 Trillion a Year to Stop Fighting Over Money. They're Suing Each Other Anyway.

## Hook

Every year, Pakistan sends roughly Rs. 8.2 trillion to its provinces through a formula
specifically designed to end the argument over who gets what [The Nation, 27 Feb 2026]. In
2026, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa is suing the federal government in Pakistan's Federal Constitutional
Court over exactly that argument — close to Rs. 964 billion it says it's owed [Dawn, "KP moves
FCC over Rs964bn in NFC dues"]. The strangest part isn't corruption, or bad faith. It's that the
formula deciding this money hasn't been rewritten in sixteen years.

## Context

The National Finance Commission (NFC) Award is the constitutional mechanism — under Article 160
— that decides how centrally collected tax revenue is split between the federal government and
the provinces, and then among the four provinces themselves [Express Tribune, "Explainer: What
is the NFC award?"]. The current formula still in use is the 7th NFC Award, agreed with
provincial consensus in May 2010 [7th NFC Award document, Government of KP Finance Department].
Before that award, the entire distribution among provinces was based on one factor: population.
The 2010 award was the first to change that.

So: what does the actual formula look like? What changed in 2018 that this formula never
caught up to? And why, sixteen years on, is a province taking the federal government to court
over it instead of a new award just being negotiated?

## Escalating narrative

### The formula, explained simply

[cut to: divisible-pool diagram]

Say the federal government collects Rs. 100 in taxes that go into the "divisible pool." Under
the 7th Award, the center keeps Rs. 42.50, and the remaining Rs. 57.50 is split among the four
provinces [7th NFC Award document]. *[for illustration only]* That second split — how the
57.50 breaks down between Punjab, Sindh, KP, and Balochistan — is where the 2010 award actually
innovated. Instead of splitting purely by population, it introduced four weighted factors:
population at 82%, poverty and backwardness at 10.3%, revenue collection and generation at 5%,
and inverse population density at 2.7% [SDPI, "NFC Award: Devising formula for horizontal
distribution"]. Population still dominates the formula — but for the first time, a province
that collected more tax, or was poorer, or was more sparsely populated, could get a little more
than pure population math would give it.

### The merger the formula never caught up to

[cut to: map — former FATA into KP, 2018]

In 2018, Pakistan's federally administered tribal areas — FATA — were formally merged into
Khyber Pakhtunkhwa as regular districts, now called the Newly Merged Tribal Districts (NMTDs)
[Express Tribune, "K-P seeks 3% NFC share for tribal districts"]. As part of that merger, KP
was promised a dedicated 3% share of the NFC divisible pool specifically to fund development in
these districts [Express Tribune; Minute Mirror]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: confirm the exact terms and
intended duration of the 3% commitment before stating it as a fixed, ongoing entitlement on
camera.]*

But the 7th NFC Award's horizontal-distribution formula was written in 2010 — eight years
before that merger existed. The formula was never rewritten to fold the merged districts'
population into KP's share the way it does for the rest of the province. Every year since,
instead of a new award being negotiated, the 7th Award has simply been extended as-is
[Pakistan Today, 29 Mar 2026, "KP Rejects Unconstitutional NFC Process"].

### Money calculated for a map that no longer exists

[cut to: before/after — same formula, different population reality]

Run the Rs. 100 example again, but now picture KP's actual share being calculated the same way
it was in 2010 — as if those merged districts, and the people living in them, still didn't
formally exist as part of the province. *[for illustration only]* That's effectively what KP
argues has been happening: because the formula was never updated, resources that should have
been allocated to the merged districts' population instead flowed elsewhere in the calculation
— to other provinces' shares [Dawn; Minute Mirror].

### What the dispute actually adds up to

[cut to: numbered figures graphic]

The numbers here are large and reported slightly differently depending on what exactly is being
measured. KP's Federal Constitutional Court petition centers on roughly Rs. 964 billion it says
should have gone to the merged districts between 2018-19 and 2025-26 but didn't [Dawn]. Separately,
against an estimated development requirement of Rs. 2.2 trillion for the merged districts over
several years, only around Rs. 1 trillion has actually been released — a shortfall exceeding
Rs. 1.2 trillion — and KP's total claims across different categories now exceed Rs. 1.6 trillion
[Minute Mirror]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: these figures — Rs964bn, Rs1.2tn, and Rs1.6tn — appear to
measure overlapping but distinct things (specific NFC-formula arrears vs. total development
funding shortfall vs. cumulative claims); reconcile against a single KP Finance Department
document before quoting one number as "the" figure on camera.]* The Federal Constitutional
Court has admitted KP's petition for hearing [Dawn].

### Why nobody's just fixed it

[cut to: presenter, direct address]

Pakistan did formally open negotiations for the 11th NFC Award, the one that's supposed to
replace the 2010 formula entirely [Arab News, "Pakistan opens talks for new national
revenue-sharing formula"]. Those talks have stalled [Dunya Digital, "Can the 11th NFC Bake a
Bigger, Fairer Pie?"]. Part of the reason is structural, not just political: the 18th
Amendment includes a clause guaranteeing that no province's share can fall below what it
received under the 2009 baseline that fed into the 2010 award [Express Tribune, "Pakistan
federation and the lie within"]. That means renegotiating the formula to properly account for
KP's merged districts isn't a clean rewrite — it's a negotiation where every other province has
a constitutional floor protecting what it already gets, so any real fix has to find new money
or ask someone else to accept a smaller slice of future growth, not their current share.

## Payoff/insight

This isn't really a story about money going missing. It's what happens when a formula gets
frozen in place for a version of the country that no longer exists, and the constitution that
was supposed to make renegotiation fair also makes it nearly impossible — because it guarantees
every province can only ever gain ground, never lose it, from where they stood in 2010. Fixing
the formula for Khyber Pakhtunkhwa's new districts doesn't just require goodwill. It requires
someone to be the first province in sixteen years to agree to something other than "more than
last time."
