# Episode: Is Pakistan Really Losing Its Multinationals? The Truth Is More Specific Than "Exodus"

## Hook

P&G. Shell. Microsoft. Uber. Pfizer. Yamaha. Every single one of these companies has been
reported as "leaving Pakistan" in just the last two years. Here's the twist: not one of them
has actually stopped selling to Pakistani consumers. So what is actually happening — and why
does it matter more, not less, once you understand what it really is?

## Context

Six major multinational names have made headlines for pulling back from Pakistan since 2023
[reporting compiled across TechJuice, Profit by Pakistan Today, ProPakistani, Bloomberg,
Dawn, and others cited below]. But "exodus" flattens six genuinely different corporate
decisions into one story. Meanwhile, something real and much less visible has been happening in
the background: Pakistan's foreign direct investment has been falling hard for three straight
years [Dawn; SGSPL; Business Standard].

So: what did each of these six companies actually do? What do they have in common once you look
past the headline? And is the government right to push back on the word "exodus" at all?

## Escalating narrative

### Ownership handovers, not exits

[cut to: ownership-transfer diagram]

Start with Shell. In June 2023, Shell's parent company told Shell Pakistan's board it intended
to divest. By October 2023, it had signed an agreement to sell its 77.42% majority stake to a
Saudi firm, Wafi Energy LLC [Express Tribune; F&L Asia]. The stated reasons were specific:
exchange losses from rupee depreciation, and a fatal tanker accident in South Punjab that cut
Shell's market share from 13% to 8% [Express Tribune]. But the Shell brand itself continues in
Pakistan under a licensing deal with the new owner — the pumps still say Shell.
*[NEEDS SOURCE: confirm the sale has fully closed — as of the sources checked, completion was
expected by Q4 2024 pending regulatory approval; verify final closing before recording.]*

Pfizer followed a similar shape. In May 2024, Pfizer sold its Karachi manufacturing facility to
Lucky Core Industries, part of the Lucky Group, and transferred much of its local pharmaceutical
portfolio with it [Profit by Pakistan Today]. Pfizer's products didn't disappear from Pakistani
pharmacies — they're now imported rather than locally manufactured.

In both cases, the company didn't leave the market. It sold the local asset to someone else and
kept selling through a different structure.

### Manufacturing shutdowns, distribution survives

[cut to: factory-to-distributor diagram]

P&G's move, announced officially on 2 October 2025, was to wind down direct manufacturing and
commercial operations in Pakistan — both P&G Pakistan and Gillette Pakistan — as part of a
global restructuring [ProPakistani; Profit by Pakistan Today; Bloomberg]. This had already
started in April 2024, when P&G sold its soap manufacturing plant in Hub, Balochistan, to Nimir
Industrial Chemicals [Wikipedia]. Going forward, P&G's products will keep reaching Pakistani
shelves through a third-party distributor model — the factory closes, the shelf doesn't.

Yamaha's exit was driven by something more directly measurable: a real collapse in demand.
Yamaha's motorcycle sales fell 57% in the first quarter of FY24-25 and 31% across the first
eight months, totalling just 3,587 units [TechJuice; PakWheels]. On 9 September 2025, Yamaha
officially ended motorcycle production in Pakistan — but promised continued spare parts,
warranty, and service support through its dealer network [ProPakistani]. Here, unlike Shell or
P&G, the driver wasn't currency or strategy — it was a market that had genuinely stopped
buying.

### Office closures under a "sell without a footprint" strategy

[cut to: presenter]

Microsoft's case is smaller in headcount than the headlines suggest. After 25 years in
Pakistan, Microsoft closed its local office in July 2025 — laying off just five employees
[Dawn; TechCrunch]. Reporting attributes this to two things at once: Pakistan's political and
economic climate, and Microsoft's own global shift toward selling cloud and SaaS products
without needing local physical offices anywhere [Dawn; Gulf News]. Microsoft says it will keep
serving Pakistani customers through resellers and neighboring regional offices.

Uber's story is really two separate events. In 2022, Uber folded its own city-level operations
into its subsidiary Careem, and by April 2024 had stopped running the Uber-branded app in
Pakistan entirely, with rides continuing under the Careem brand [ANI; BusinessToday]. Then, in
2025, Careem itself announced it would suspend ride-hailing operations across Pakistan from 18
July, citing "the challenging macroeconomic reality, intensifying competition, and global
capital allocation" [Careem CEO Mudassir Sheikha, via Khaleej Times/Mettis Global]. Even here,
Careem's other businesses — food and grocery delivery, payments — keep operating in Pakistan
[Islamabad Scene].

### What's actually real behind all six

[cut to: FDI trendline graphic]

Here's what none of these six stories capture on their own: Pakistan's foreign direct
investment fell 33% in FY26 to $1.195 billion, continuing declines from $1.92 billion in
FY23-24 and $1.83 billion in FY24-25 [SGSPL; Dawn]. Reporting attributes this to rupee
depreciation eroding margins, inconsistent tax policy, and regulatory complexity — with one
analyst quoted as saying investors "fear bureaucracy rather than risk" [Business Recorder /
South Asia Press]. Other multinationals beyond the six covered here — Telenor, Eni, and some
foreign banks among them — have also been reported scaling back over the same period
[South Asia Press, Oct 2025] *[NEEDS SOURCE: these additional cases weren't independently
verified company-by-company here the way the six above were — confirm before naming them
on camera.]*

But the government pushes back on the word "exodus" itself. An adviser to the Finance Minister,
Khurram Schehzad, has said these exits reflect companies "changing their global strategies,"
not a Pakistan-specific flight — and points to 79 new foreign entrants against 19 exits between
2023 and 2025 [South Asia Press, Oct 2025]. That stat is real, but it doesn't tell us the size
or sector of those 79 new entrants, so it can be true at the same time FDI dollars are falling —
more companies arriving doesn't necessarily mean more money arriving.

## Payoff/insight

None of these six companies actually abandoned the Pakistani market. Each one restructured its
ownership, its manufacturing, or its physical footprint — for six genuinely different, verified
reasons: currency losses, a factory sale, a strategic shift to distributors, a real collapse in
demand, a global SaaS pivot, and a macroeconomic bet on ride-hailing not being worth it anymore.
But stack all six next to a real, three-year FDI decline, and the pattern underneath is the
same in every case: it's become cheaper and safer for multinationals to sell into Pakistan from
the outside than to be financially exposed inside it.
