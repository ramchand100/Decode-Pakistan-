# Episode: Pakistan "Solved" Circular Debt in 2025 — So Why Is It Still Growing?

## Hook

In 2025, eighteen of Pakistan's biggest banks signed what was called a landmark deal:
Rs. 1.225 trillion to finally resolve the power sector's circular debt [Pakistan Banks
Association, 2025]. Government officials called it historic. The IMF signed off on it. Case
closed, right? Except in the very next fiscal year, circular debt grew by another Rs. 364
billion anyway [Express Tribune / Shafaqna, reporting FY2025-26 data]. If the problem was
actually solved, that's not supposed to happen. So what did that "landmark deal" actually fix —
and what does it mean that the debt kept growing right through it?

## Context

Circular debt is the chain of unpaid bills that runs through Pakistan's entire power sector:
distribution companies (DISCOs) can't fully collect what they bill consumers, so they can't
fully pay the Central Power Purchasing Agency, which can't fully pay power producers, who then
can't fully pay their fuel suppliers. Money owed at one end of the chain becomes an unpaid bill
everywhere downstream of it [general mechanism, corroborated across CRSS, ADB, and IEEFA
reporting].

By early 2026, this had built up into a stock of roughly Rs. 2.4 trillion — about 2.1% of
Pakistan's entire GDP [Pakistan Banks Association, 2025]. That's the debt the 2025 bank deal
was supposed to deal with.

So: what did that deal actually do? Why did the debt keep growing anyway? And who ends up
paying for all of it?

## Escalating narrative

### The chain nobody sees on their bill

[cut to: mechanism diagram — consumer to DISCO to CPPA-G to IPP to fuel supplier]

Say a DISCO bills its customers Rs. 100 worth of electricity in a month. Between theft, faulty
meters, unpaid government and industrial accounts, and tariffs set below actual cost, it
actually collects only Rs. 85. *[for illustration only]* That missing Rs. 15 doesn't just
disappear from a spreadsheet — it becomes the DISCO's own unpaid bill to CPPA-G, the agency
that buys power on DISCOs' behalf and pays it out to power producers [CRSS; ADB circular debt
documentation]. Multiply that gap across every DISCO in the country, every month, for years,
and you get the debt stock.

### The line on your bill you've never noticed

[cut to: shocking-stat graphic — Rs14.3 vs Rs9 per unit]

Here's the part almost nobody clocks. In FY25, Pakistani consumers paid roughly Rs. 1.81
trillion in capacity charges alone — about Rs. 14.3 per unit. The actual energy that was
generated and used cost only around Rs. 9 per unit [IEEFA, capacity payments reporting]. That
means the single biggest cost on your electricity bill often isn't the electricity you actually
used — it's a fixed fee tied to how much generation *capacity exists*, whether it's running or
not. This comes from what are called "take-or-pay" contracts with independent power
producers (IPPs): Pakistan agreed to pay for a fixed share of a plant's capacity regardless of
demand [PACRA IPP rating criteria; IEEFA]. Reformers are now pushing IPPs, including several
Chinese-backed ones, to switch to "take-and-pay" terms — paying only for electricity actually
delivered — as a condition attached to unlocking part of the 2025 debt facility [EnergyUpdate,
18 May 2026]. But most existing contracts still run on the old terms.

### What the "landmark deal" actually did

[cut to: before/after diagram — debt stock, then debt reclassified]

Now here's where the 2025 deal gets interesting — and where the word "resolved" starts doing a
lot of work it probably shouldn't. Of the Rs. 2.4 trillion circular debt stock, Rs. 1.225
trillion was borrowed from those eighteen commercial banks specifically to pay off older,
higher-interest arrears owed to power producers and to retire loans held by Power Holding
Limited, the government company set up to warehouse this debt [Pakistan Banks Association,
2025]. That bank loan isn't being repaid by the government out of thin air — it's repaid over
six years through a dedicated Debt Service Surcharge added directly onto every consumer's
electricity bill, set at 10% of NEPRA's determined revenue requirement [Pakistan Banks
Association, 2025].

So the "resolution" didn't erase the debt. It converted informal, overdue arrears into a
formal, interest-bearing bank loan — and moved the repayment onto a specific new line item on
your bill for the next six years. The debt didn't vanish. It got a new lender and a new
collection mechanism: you.

### Why it kept growing anyway

[cut to: numbered breakdown graphic]

And that's exactly why circular debt could grow again right through a trillion-rupee
restructuring: the 2025 deal dealt with the *stock* — the backlog already built up — without
fixing the *flow* — the same causes that keep generating new debt every single month.
Reporting on the FY2025-26 buildup points to several of those causes at once: continuing DISCO
inefficiencies and losses, weaker-than-expected bill recovery, unpaid dues from K-Electric, and
delays in passing tariff adjustments through to consumers on schedule [Shafaqna / Express
Tribune, FY2025-26 circular debt reporting]. *[NEEDS SOURCE: the government's Rs. 302 billion in
subsidies that fiscal year were meant to offset some of this — the reported gross contributing
factors and the final net Rs364bn figure don't cleanly reconcile in public reporting; verify
against a primary Ministry of Finance or NEPRA breakdown before quoting exact rupee splits on
camera.]*

Those are largely the same structural weaknesses that existed before the bank deal was ever
signed. Restructuring old debt doesn't stop a DISCO from under-collecting next month's bills.

### The lever the government is actually pulling

[cut to: presenter, direct address]

This is also exactly why the government is now trying to privatize three of the DISCOs —
FESCO, GEPCO, and IESCO — the subject of our last episode. But as we covered there, those are
the three DISCOs with the *lowest* losses, not the ones actually driving the bulk of the
problem. The worst-performing distribution companies, and the take-or-pay contracts behind
those capacity payments, are still sitting exactly where they were.

## Payoff/insight

Put the bank deal and the privatization push together, and the pattern is the same in both
cases: Pakistan is restructuring who holds the *existing* debt and who owns the
*best-performing* assets — while the two things that actually generate new circular debt every
year, DISCO losses and take-or-pay capacity payments, stay largely untouched. A "resolution"
that doesn't change the flow isn't a resolution. It's a repayment plan — and for now, it's one
you're paying off on your electricity bill, six years at a time.
